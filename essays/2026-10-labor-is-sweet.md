# Labor is sweet

US Labor Day, 2026

![](https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80,width=1920,height=3840/uploads/asset/file/95e98b02-e0d8-496e-8f97-7f42ef35c238/image.png?t=1788804817)

[The Harvesters, Pieter Bruegel the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Harvesters_%28painting%29)

It’s Labor Day in the United States. Here in Michigan, it’s a fine day, but Bess and I are down with slight colds and unable to attend the Labor Day singing in Okemos that Bob Borcherding and Anne Missavage put on every year. Ora por nobis, us poor souls.

But napping, I began to wonder about which occupations are mentioned in 2025 edition of *The Sacred Harp*. Who are the laborers we sing about?

Let me mention the *shepherds* watching their flocks by night, all seated on the ground (186 SHERBURNE). They are told to rejoice and lift up their eyes (152 SHEPHERDS REJOICE, 306 OXFORD) and worship the Babe in the manger (51 STAR IN THE EAST). Of course, the *angels* are pretty active, too — the Greek word originally simple meant “messenger,” but I will limit myself to human laborers – but an essay on angels in *The Sacred Harp* might not be a bad idea. But along with the shepherds, we have *watchmen* in 259 EASTON: “What dost thou see, O watchman?” espying the “rising of a star” —at least, I think so. It’s a slightly confusing text.

Next, we have *soldiers*, mostly as an analogy. “Am I a soldier of the cross?” asks Isaac Watts (57 CHRISTIAN SOLDIER, 242 REVERE, 309 LIVING LAMB, 325 SOLDIER OF THE CROSS). But with Jesus as our Captain going before, we “put on gospel armor/of faith and hope and love” and follow him as brave soldiers of peace (82t BOUND FOR CANAAN, 85 THE MORNING TRUMPET), fighting against, and conquering, “sin and sorrow.” We are soldiers “in the good old way” (213t THE GOOD OLD WAY).

But there are literal solders mentioned, too (346 THE AMERICAN STAR):

The spirits of Washington, Warren, Montgomery  
Look down from the clouds with bright aspect serene.  
Come, *soldiers*, a tear and a toast to their mem’ry,  
Rejoicing they’ll see us as they once have been.

And *sailors*, too. Literal ones, like the *seamen* in danger of perishing (224 SAVE, LORD, OR WE PERISH), but we are all called happy sailors in 388 THE HAPPY SAILOR — again, with Jesus, “Judah’s Lion,” as our captain.

Of course, *ministers* and *preachers* are well represented. They’re saying goodbye as they are leaving, or dying, or both (69t MINISTER’S FAREWELL, 83b THE DYING MINISTER, 341 THE LONE PILGRIM, 348t AINSLIE, 347t CHRISTIAN’S FAREWELL, 385b CAN I LEAVE YOU?). But we also encourage them to “Go, preachers, and tell it to the world” that “through free grace and a dying Lamb/poor mourners found a home at last.”

*Servants* show up a lot — nine songs by my count. Usually, this is by analogy, for example, “servants of the heavenly King,” in 31b WEBSTER and 52t ALBION and “servants of my God” in 540 NIDRAH. But the actual role of a servant is found in 113 THE PRODIGAL SON, where the eponymous son declares, “Not worthy to be called his son/I’ll ask a servant’s place.”

The unusual job, these days, of *prophet* shows up a lot. In 49b MEAR, we mourn that “no prophet speaks to calm our grief” these days. But we also look back to the time of the prophets: “The way the holy prophet(s) went” (53 JERUSALEM, 70b SAVE, MIGHTY LORD), and their prophecies, for example: “The time that prophets were foretelling,/With signs and wonders now is come.”(102 FULFILLMENT). Isaiah, the Old Testament prophet, is name-checked in 232 BAPTISMAL ANTHEM. John the Baptist (job title? perhaps, but he was also a prophet) is also mentioned therein and in 572 THE LAMB OF GOD.

But it’s 117 BABYLON IS FALLEN that has the most mentions of occupations:

Hail the day so long expected,  
Hail the year of full release:  
Zion’s walls are now erected,  
And her *watchmen* publish peace.  
Through our Shiloh’s wide dominion,  
Hear the trumpet loudly roar;  
Babylon is fallen, is fallen, is fallen,  
Babylon is fallen to rise no more.

All her *merchants* stand with wonder,  
“What is this that comes to pass?”  
Murm’ring like the distant thunder,  
Crying, “O alas, alas!”  
Swell the sound, ye *kings* and *nobles*,  
*Priests* and people, rich and poor;  
Babylon is fallen, is fallen, is fallen,  
Babylon is fallen to rise no more.

Speaking of kings and nobles, we have *princes* mentioned in 162 PLENARY. And don’t forget “the trembling jailor” of 59 HOLY MANNA!

I’d be remiss to ignore all the titles and occupations that God is given, which are too many to cite by individual song. But God is called our *advocate*, *captain*, *judge*, *king*, *physician*, *priest,* *prophet*, and *shepherd*. And, is *God* a title, too, in addition to a name/rigid designator? I think so. God is God, and so, “let the *Creator’s* praise arise.”

Labor and laboring is almost always viewed favorably in the texts of *The Sacred Harp*. Sometimes the goodness of labor is described directly, but often used for the work of the Christian: prayer and witness, especially. In the words of 347t CHRISTIAN’S FAREWELL:

Here I have worked, labored awhile,  
But labor is sweet if Jesus doth smile.  
When I am done, I will go home  
Where Jesus is smiling  
And bids me to come.

(Now playing 338 *SAWYER*’S EXIT).
