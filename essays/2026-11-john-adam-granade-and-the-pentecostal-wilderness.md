# John Adam Granade and the Pentecostal Wilderness

Poor sinners are returning home

![](https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80,width=1920,height=3840/uploads/asset/file/ba741e51-ff08-4f8b-a952-b53b42c30285/IMG_3254.jpeg?t=1791248843)

‎⁨South Bay⁩, ⁨Lake Nipigon⁩, ⁨Macdiarmid⁩, ⁨Ontario⁩, ⁨Canada⁩

In 1797, John Adam Granade was invited to go with revivalist William Burke on a circuit of camp meetings. Granade was convinced of his deep brokenness, but not of his salvation. That winter, he spent a lot of time “howling and roaring for mercy.” In August, 1800, that sense of forgiveness came. John Carr, a Methodist preacher, described what happened:

> The scene was awful and solemn beyond description. It drew the attention of the hundreds of people assembled on the ground, and the clergy as well as the laity were struck with wonder, while they witnessed a change, the like of which had never before come under their notice. Heaven was pictured on the face of the happy man, and his language, as though learned in a new world, was apparently superhuman. He spoke of angels and archangels, cherubim and seraphim, and dwelt with rapture upon the fulness and freeness of the gospel of Christ for the salvation of a lost world \[^1\].

It is not surprising that Granade got the nickname “the wild man of Goose Creek.” He went on to become a circuit “exhorter.” He was, in fact, a leader in what became known as “shouting Methodism.” Bishop Thomas Coke described a 1790 service in Baltimore:

> Out of a congregation of two thousand people, I supposed two or three hundred were engaged at the same time in praising God, praying for the conviction and conversion of sinners, or exhorting those there with the utmost vehemence. And hundreds more were engaged in wrestling prayer either for their own conversion or sanctification\[^2\].

This was not the suburban Methodism of my father’s church.

Granade worked so hard and shouted so hard that his health began to fail. In 1803 — just a few years later — he withdrew from the circuit to study medicine. But his health failed, and he died in 1807, leaving a pregnant wife and three children.

Granade contributed some of the wildest texts found in the 2025 *Sacred Harp*. The only mention of *dragons*, *fiery serpents*, and *hurricanes*, for example, found in 121 FLORENCE:

Though storms and hurricanes arise,  
The desert all around,  
And fiery serpents oft appear  
Through the enchanted ground,  
Dark nights and clouds and gloomy fear,  
And dragons often roar,  
But while the gospel trump we hear,  
We’ll press for Canaan’s shore.

The wildness of 116 HOOPER is found in its meter as much as in its images:

When Jesus first found me to hell I was going,  
His love did surround me and saved me from ruin;  
He kindly embraced me, and sweetly He kissed me,  
And taught me aloud His sweet praises to sing.

No wonder its meter is marked as “P.M.” — particular meter, meaning it’s meter is “particular” or “peculiar” to this text. In a world of common meter poets, Granade was his own man.

Granade’s images are of difficult weather (“Jordon’s stormy waves”, “chilling winds and beating rains, ” “waters deep and cold”), difficult journeys (“trav’lers through the wilderness”), spiritual warfare (“Take courage and fight valiantly / Stand fast with sword in hand”), and loud trumpets (“the gospel trump we hear”, “Gabriel’s trump”).

And, of course, shouting. He found himself in the midst of a great and marvelous revival, which brought many Christian sects together, crossing age, gender, and racial barriers. He found himself in what might be the last days. It was *messy*\[^3\], but wonderful. In the words of 102 FULFILLMENT:

See how the Scriptures are fulfilling,  
Poor sinners are returning home;  
The time that prophets were foretelling,  
With signs and wonders now is come.  
The gospel trumpets now are blowing  
From sea to sea, from land to land;  
God’s Holy Spirit down is pouring,  
And Christians joining heart and hand.

Ten thousand fall before Jehovah,  
For mercy, mercy! loud they cry;  
They rise, all shouting “Hallelujah!”  
And “Glory be to God on high.”  
But many cry, “It’s all disorder,”  
And disbelieve God’s holy word;  
Yet Christians sing and shout the louder,  
“All glory, glory to the Lord.”

On the day that Granade was converted, he found himself whispering “Adoration to God and the Lamb,” and eventually crying out, “Glory to God! Glory and adoration to God and the Lamb forever!\[^4\]”

At the end of his days, I hope the words of 61 SWEET RIVERS gave him comfort:

My raptured soul shall drink and feast  
In love’s unbounded sea;  
The glorious hope of endless rest  
Is ravishing for me.

May we all reach “Canaan’s happy shore” to sing and shout with John Adam Granade.
