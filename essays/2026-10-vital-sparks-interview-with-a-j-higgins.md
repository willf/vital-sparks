# Vital Sparks: Interview with A.J. Higgins

“I’m Bound to Die in the Army: The Interplay of Language and Culture in Antebellum Hymnody”

What happened was this: I was planning to write a Vital Sparks column on the camp meeting songs in the 2025 edition of *The Sacred Harp*, when A.J. Higgins, who recently graduated with a music composition degree from Cedarville University, announced he’d written a research paper which covered similar ground. Now, never in my life have I interviewed anyone, but I thought I would interview A.J. He was game, and so this is an edited transcript of our conversation. His article is entitled “[I’m Bound to Die in the Army: The Interplay of Language and Culture in Antebellum Hymnody](https://cedarville.tind.io/record/56133?ln=en&v=pdf).”

[A.J. Higgins](https://antoniojhiggins.com)

*Will*: A.J., tell us a little bit about yourself.

*A.J.*: Great. I was born in Oklahoma. I'm a pastor's kid, so I grew up singing church songs, church music, hymns, worship songs, the whole nine yards. I grew up with it, fell in love with it. Loved researching it. I've been singing since I was like three years old, ever since I learned to read. Basically, I would sing a random song in Walmart, and that actually was kind of cool because people would be like, "Hey, can you sing at the nursing home or the senior citizen center?" I was like five, but they wanted me to sing.

*Will*: I bet you were so cute.

*A.J.*: That was part of the appeal. I've been singing for a long time. I've been engrossed in church music for a very long time. But I never thought to make it my career. I always wanted to go into creative writing as a kid, or some sort of high level science field. Those were the two options that I really thought. This is cool, but over time, I just kept on doing music, and then when that senior year hits in high school, and you're like, “What am I gonna do with my life?” You know, I had all these options that I thought I wanted to do, and I don't really want to do any of them. And of course, then people ask, “Well, why don't you just become a pastor like your dad?” And I go, “I don't really want to do that.” You know, and thinking about it, and you hear the voice, "music composition”, and you go, “Yeah, I think I could do that.” Granted, I didn't really even know if that was a degree option or a major at any school, but I looked it up, and there were some options. We narrowed it down. Cedarville was one of the schools that came up as a good school for music composition, and so I was like, “Well, I'll apply. I'll audition.

And you need to audition as an instrumentalist or a vocalist. I auditioned as a vocalist and got accepted. The first thing that happened was being exposed to a whole different side of music that I had never truly invested in—the classical music tradition. I played piano as a kid. I grew up with piano lessons, and they were classical, but other than that, I was listening to Southern gospel or J-pop or things like that—a really hodgepodge of more pop-ish influence than classical. And so that was a really difficult experience to grapple with and figure out. There's a whole brand new world.

I was slowly guided towards teaching. I really want to credit my music history professor, Dr. \[Sandra\] Yang. I always loved history since I was a kid, but I thought it was just music history. It would be an easy A class. I thought it wouldn't be that useful for a composition major, but I'd do it. And as soon as we got in there, I loved it, and I was like, I'm gonna be doing this for the rest of my life. And so, in thinking about career plans, I was like, okay, music history teacher. So that'll be university level. That'll mean I have to get a master's degree. Let's figure that out. And so, still in the process of that and figuring things out. I have a school that I have in mind, and I'm talking with the faculty there. I've been accepted, but we're still talking.

*Will*: When did you start singing Sacred Harp?

*A.J.* I started singing Sacred Harp my junior year of college, but I got really into it. We have a class at Cedarville called Global Music. Doctor Yang teaches it, and we had representatives from the Dayton Sacred Harp Group and the Cincinnati Sacred Harp Group come and give a lesson for us. And I fell in love with it, and started joining the Dayton Singers. And the rest is history.

*Will*: Lovely. That's great. So I want to turn to your paper.

You wrote a paper titled “[I’m Bound to Die in the Army: The Interplay of Language and Culture in Antebellum Hymnody](http://).” It was published in *Musical Offerings*, which is published by your Department of Music and Worship at Cedarville University. It's an undergraduate musicology journal. What brought you to write this paper?

*A.J.*: The music history classes at Cedarville require us to write a term paper, basically a mini dissertation. We don't have to present it, but we have to write the paper, and since music history split up into two courses, most schools do it in three, but Cedarville does it in two, and so music until 1800 and music after 1800. For the first year I was like, well, I found this newfound interest in Sacred Harp music. I might as well put it into a paper, and so for the first semester I just focused on William Billings, Daniel Reed, Amos Pillsbury, and figure out what's the origins of their style? So go back to the psalmody of England and talking about that and Ravenscroft and all that. And then for the second semester, I was like, now I can finally explore something else related to sacred harp that's more Sacred Harp focused. I was first going to do something about fuging tunes. But I decided, let's focus on the wording. Let's focus on the language and how that relates to the culture. And is there any cross between, because this is right in the middle of the Civil War, is there language in these songs that kind of reflects the sentiment of the Civil War? And I found, yeah, there's a lot that.

These soldiers could latch onto in these songs, and that's probably one of the reasons why these songs became that this songbook was preserved. Because these soldiers are singing, “Till the warfare is over, hallelujah!”, and it's like they're meaning that there's something there that's stirring within them that's causing them to sing these songs and continue this legacy. I really found that really interesting. The result was “I'm Bound to Die in the Army.”

*Will*: Following up on that, what do you think is the basic claim in your paper?

*A.J.*: I think the idea that Sacred Harp, the book itself, isn't in a vacuum, because we tend to hear this is a uniquely American style of music, and it is, but it's in this conflict of the mid 1800s. This conflict over slavery, over states' rights, over all these things, we have settlers moving westward. We have the camp meetings. We have a kind of new culture really developing in the American South. That's a hodgepodge of different things. We have people immigrating, and we have people coming from Europe, plantations, and we have this whole, whole, whole little universe in the South. That’s where Sacred Harp comes from, and that we should look back to and see why. Why do we sing the songs we do, and how does that relate to the actual history.

*Will*: So maybe I misunderstood a little bit about what you're getting at. Do you think that the central claim is that White Southerners, who were white Confederate ex-soldiers or people directly affected by the war—which would be practically everyone—were the main tradition bearers?

*A.J.*: No. I'm just saying, there's something that they latched onto, and it's these camp meeting songs, and then they carry that forward in singing, and it turns into a southern tradition.

*Will*: What is a camp meeting song?

*A.J.*: I think a lot of it has to do with David W. Music called it homespun music and these homespun phrases, and the camp meeting, and the camp meeting still exists today in holiness churches. It's this kind of meeting place where people go and meet. They hear sermons. They meet together, kind of like a revival service, and it started in the south in Kentucky. We had Presbyterians and Baptists and Methodists doing these, and it'd be these traveling preachers. They'd set up meetings and they'd preach, and generally the singing would be this really ecstatic form of singing, where they have these interjections like 'glory hallelujah' and 'amen,' and the songs would be Isaac Watts, Charles Wesley, Caleb Taylor. These English and New American hymn writers, who were writing these great hymns, writing these great poems, these lines, and these are being grabbed a hold of by these camp meeting services, and they're starting to add these interpolations. I think it was Ellen Jane Lorenz who calls them the mother hymn. For example, we have 277 ANTIOCH. First you have “I know that my Redeemer lives,” and then “Glory, hallelujah!” and then, “What comfort this sweet sentence gives”, and then “Glory, hallelujah!” And we just continue on from there. We have these “glory hallelujahs” interspersed within the lines of the hymn. The idea of the chorus is that even if you don't know the song we're singing, you can latch onto the chorus.

*Will*: So I just want to situate the camp meetings in time. Could you talk a little bit about when the camp meetings were first starting?

*A.J.* Sure. I talk about this a little bit in my paper. So this is in Kentucky, around the early eighteen hundreds. We had these revival services, these awakening services with George Whitfield and other Puritan and Methodist preachers. As you go down further south, a lot of these places didn't have churches. Churches hadn't been built yet in places like Kentucky and Missouri, and so they had to find ways to improvise these church services.

*Will*: So early eighteen hundreds. We are talking forty, fifty, sixty years before the Civil War. That's interesting. I think.

*A.J.*: Yes. So they had already heard these songs. There had already been something built up, and I say in my paper that we have records of Sacred Harp songs being written before the Civil War. The book was written and published in 1844, and after the Civil War, but as far as I could tell, looking at timelines, there's no song that we know of from that window of the Civil War.

*Will*: It's interesting. Maybe they had other things to do.

*A.J.*: They had other things going on. They were singing Sacred Harp. And several Sacred Harp composers were Confederate soldiers, or were training Confederate soldiers, or had high Confederate leanings.

*Will*: So I, you talk a little bit about Black or African American contributions at the meeting. So I obviously they were not Confederate soldiers or supported generally not of the. So talk a little bit more about Black contributions to the camp meeting.

*A.J.*: Sure. It was kind of similar to the African American spirituals, which also have this language of “glory, hallelujah.” A lot of these stock phrases talk about heaven. We have “religion is a fortune, heaven is a home,” “on the other side of Jordan”, “soon my troubles will be over.” These phrases get picked up or maybe these phrases were introduced by the African American population

But I'll talk about how the white settlers used the language and how that compares to how the slave population used the language. We have this new culture that's being developed in the South. And it’s more secular. Think of it. There's drinking. There's gambling. There's these vices, and there's these Christians who say, "Well, we have a different goal in what we're doing here." One that differed significantly from those of the secular southern world. They thought, we're here, we're the the new Canaan. We're the new Israel. Kind of vestiges of these Puritan concepts, and they had this divine promise, and that kind of set them apart from their peers in the South. And so that's kind of how they use this language broadly. And then on the other side, this language is getting picked up by African American slaves. They're thinking about freedom, heaven. Heaven is a free place. So we have “there's a better day acomin'. Will you go along with me? There's a better day acomin'. Go and sound the jubilee,” So this language of jubilee of heaven is being picked up and being reinterpreted to talk about freedom.

*Will*: So the basic development is we have camp meeting songs developed in camp meetings in the early eighteen hundreds. Developed by both white and black singers. They have this sense of the mother hymn, which is sort of the base hymn of the song, and then you have these various interjections, revival refrains and so forth. As that music gets used during the Civil War in the South, we sort of take the prototypical white Southerner as a soldier. He is literally fighting a battle and also fighting for this sort of new ideal of what their nation should be. But on the other hand, you have the enslaved population who see the messages of freedom and so forth that's being promised there, and so they get that.

*A.J.*: We also have union soldiers. What are they singing? And we have union soldiers who are freedmen or slaves that have escaped and have joined the union army to get their freedom and they're singing these songs too. For example, we have the [First Colored Infantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1st_United_States_Colored_Infantry_Regiment) marching song. And it has that line: “Who ordered to a battle / Take Jesus as our guide.” They're fighting for our freedom.They're in that battle, and so they want our Lord to go with them in that battle.

*Will*: We sing these camp meeting songs today. There are all kinds of people who sing this song, this music now. What would you would like a Sacred Harp singer to keep in mind as they sing these camp meeting songs?

*A.J.*: That's a really good question. I think that we have to again take in this historical context, as in how are people in the past singing these songs? We have white settlers, we have Union soldiers, we have Confederate soldiers, we have the African enslaved population. How are we connected to that? Those might be questions we need to grapple with. How are people thinking about the words? Because this is both music and text, and they pair together in a really unique way. And how are we going to grapple with that, as people who sing these songs in the present? Does that kind of make sense?

*Will*: Certainly makes sense to me. Absolutely.

By the way, I’m not a musicologist, but I have been an academic at parts of my life, and I thought, when I read your paper, “It's really good for an undergraduate paper.” So, thank you. So I encourage you — I don't know what God wants you to do with your life, but you show some promise in this area.

Thank you very much for your time, and I hope to sing with you sometime.

*A.J.*: That'll be great.
