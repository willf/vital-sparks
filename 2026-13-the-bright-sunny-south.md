# The Bright, Sunny South

Secular songs in The Cooper Book, 2012

![](https://media.beehiiv.com/cdn-cgi/image/fit=scale-down,format=auto,onerror=redirect,quality=80,width=1920,height=3840/uploads/asset/file/f141651f-84c6-491b-982b-0fae0b52cfb3/image.png?t=1791992540)

Texas Wendish Heritage Museum, photo by the author

I don’t get to sing from *The Sacred Harp 2012 Cooper Book Edition* often enough, to be honest. I generally go to Syble Adams’s “birthday singing” in October, and that’s always a delightful time (well, except the parting hand this year, when there was an *unfortunate incident* when vertigo overcame me. Syble’s son saved the day by quickly bringing a trash can).

Like the 2025 edition of *The Sacred Harp,* the *Cooper Book* is almost completely a Christian songbook. In both cases, the small number of secular songs prevents it, perhaps, from being a “hymnal” *per se*. But as I was singing at Syble’s, and contemplating afterwards, I noticed a couple of differences in the number and kinds of secular songs in the *Cooper Book*. There are many songs that appear in both books, including patriotic songs like LIBERTY, MOUNT VERNON, and MURILLO’S LESSON. Both books share *memento mori* songs that remind us to live well in the light of our “certain doom,” such as CALVARY. Both books have parting songs like CAN I LEAVE YOU?

Yes, my native land, I love thee,  
All thy scenes I love them well,  
Friends, connections, happy country,  
Can I bid you all farewell?

And both have sentimental ballads, like THE DYING BOY, although the *Cooper Book* adds a second verse that speaks of heaven:

I’m dying, mother, dying now,  
Please raise my aching head,  
And fan my heated, burning brow,  
Your boy will soon be dead.  
Turn o’er my pillow once again,  
And kiss my fevered cheek,  
I’ll soon be freed from all the pain,  
For now I am so weak.

A band of angels beckons me,  
I can no longer stay;  
Hark! how they sing, ‘We welcome thee;  
Dear brother, haste away.’  
The hour has come, my end is near,  
My soul is mounting high’r;  
What glorious strains salute my ear,  
From heav’n’s angelic choir?[1]

## Angels

Ok, about that “band of angels.” Angels are mentioned in 77 songs in the *Cooper Book*, and only 56 times in the 2025 edition — 12.5% songs of the *Cooper* vs. 9.5% of the songs in the 2025 edition. Some of these songs are what I think of as semi-religious — not exactly Christian, and not exactly non-Christian. I’ll start with 463 ANGEL BAND,[2] perhaps the most well-known song, although it does mention more or less explicit Christian concepts (“holy ranks” and “Jordan’s bank” and “heav’nly home”):

My latest sun is sinking fast,  
My race is nearly run;  
My strongest trials now are past,  
My triumph is begun.

O come, angel band,  
Come and around me stand;  
O bear me away on your snowy wings,  
To my immortal home.

The idea of a band of angels bearing the dead to heaven — this probably derives from Jesus’s parable of the rich man and Lazarus, in which, in Jesus’s telling, Lazarus the beggar dies and he is “carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom.” (Luke 16:22) I think this understanding is more than the parable can itself bear.

In 540 SHE IS SLEEPING, the Greek myth of Charon, the ferryman to Hades, is Christianized:

Come, O boatman, row me over,  
To a fairer land than this;  
For my heart grows weary waiting,  
For my angel mother’s kiss.

The narrator’s mother died and passed over with the same boatman at the end of her “Christian’s course.” And it’s strongly implied that she has become an angel. Strict Christian theology suggests that humans and angels are two separate “orders of creation.[3] ” But this has, apparently, been a folk belief from early days, perhaps based on earlier Jewish ideas or the statement that people will be like the angels with respect to marriage (Matthew 22:30)[4] . Beautiful and sentimental thoughts, though.

## More sentimental ballads and patriotic songs

Returning to sentimental ballads, 436b THE INFANT REQUEST is completely secular in nature:

For just before the fatal hour,  
In which cold death did nip the flower,  
He begged me — and it makes me weep —  
To sing my little one to sleep.

The flower budded, hope was strong,  
But hope could not its life prolong;  
For soon, too soon, alas! I’m made  
To see it blossom but to fade.

And 506 THE ORPHAN GIRL is a kind of pair to the THE BLIND GIRL in the 2025 edition:

‘No home, no home,’ plead a little girl,  
At the door of a princely hall,  
As she trembling stood on the polished step,  
And leaned on the marble wall.

At least the orphan girl flees to a nebulous “home above / where there’s room and bread for the poor.”

And returning to patriotic songs, we have a state-based one, 422t A SONG OF TEXAS:

Away here in Texas, the bright, Sunny South,  
The cold storms of winter defy;  
The dark, lurid clouds that envelop the North,  
Scarce darken our beautiful sky.

Away here in Texas, the sun shines so bright,  
The stars in their beauty appear;  
The full moon in splendor illumines the night,  
And the seasons roll round through the year.

Living in Michigan, I know “the dark, lurid clouds” well, and love them — but to each their choice. It would be nice to see the stars more.

The text to 582 LONG AGO, COMRADES is in the style of the 2025 edition’s 346 THE AMERICAN STAR. It’s a soldier’s ode, “set to *When you and I were young, Maggie*” to younger days as Confederate soldiers:

Long ago we were boys in the ranks, comrades,  
Our hearts light, and happy as the day,  
Cheeks were ruddy, eyes bright, locks were dark, comrades,  
As we marched from our home far away.

## “Dropped” songs and their relatives

There are a number of songs in the *Cooper Book* that were present in the 1991 edition (the “Denson book” or the “red book”) that were dropped from the 2025 edition (the new, green book) that are more or less secular in nature.

It is debatable whether 254 ROSE OF SHARON should be considered just a love song, because it is based on the Song of Solomon in the Bible. I discussed this in the essay [Ye heedless ones: Secular songs in *The Sacred Harp*, Part 2](https://vitalsparks.beehiiv.com/p/vital-sparks-ye-heedless-ones). It talks of love and apples and banqueting houses and weather, but never mentions God. Another song based on the Song of Solomon is 446a SHARON’S LOVELY ROSE.

In Sharon’s lovely rose  
Immortal beauties shine;  
Its sweet, refreshing fragrance shows  
Its origin divine.

How blooming and how fair!  
O may my happy breast  
This lovely rose forever wear,  
And be supremely blest.

If I came across this text without its *Cooper* book context, I would have guessed it was a Marian hymn. Another variant is the fancy anthem 188 SPRING, with its “lovely vernal flow’rs” and “warbling choirs[5] ,” also from the Song of Solomon, and also dropped from the 2025 edition.

The temperance song 334 O COME AWAY! was also dropped from the 2025 edition — I discussed this in [“O Come, Come Away” is gone away](https://vitalsparks.beehiiv.com/p/update-o-come-come-away-is-gone-away). Its words are essentially secular. It doesn’t even mention a Higher Power. although “heaven’s blessings” are invoked on the alcoholic’s path to sobriety.

O come, come away,  
From labor now reposing,  
Our jubilee has set us free—  
O come, come away!  
Come, hail the day that celebrates  
The ransom of th’inebriates  
From all that does intoxicate,  
O come, come away!

And a version of *Du, du, liegst mir im Herzen*, 323b SOFT MUSIC, which praises the power of music, stayed in the Cooper book, but left the 2025 edition.

## A song of blessing

Like the 2025 edition, *The Sacred Harp 2012 Cooper Book Edition* is largely a Christian tunebook, and thus even its more secular songs should be considered in that context. Let me finish with a song of what must be considered a song of blessing, although, strictly considered, it could be sung without concern in a secular setting. I am talking of 574 SWEET PEACE, and may its blessing apply to you.

May grace and truth,  
Sweet peace and rest  
Dwell in each breast,  
Sweet peace and rest.
