# On Jordan’s stormy banks I stand

## Samuel Stennett

There are five separate poems, used in nine different songs, by Samuel Stennett in the 2025 edition of _The Sacred Harp_ [^sacred-harp-songs]. Stennett (1727–1795) was a Baptist minister, from a line of Baptist ministers. His great-grandfather, Edward Stennett, led an illegally assembled house church in the reign of Charles II. His grandfather, Joseph, filled the position of pastor of a sabbatarian Baptist congregation when the previous pastor was jailed for sedition. Joseph was also a poet and friend of Nahum Tate’s [^joseph-stennett-source].

Samuel took over his father’s role as the pastor of Little Wild Street Baptist Church in London; he was simultaneously the pastor of the same sabbatarian Baptist Church[^pinners-hall]. He thus belonged to the second or third generation of hymn writers pioneered by his great-grandfather and by Isaac Watts in the Dissident tradition. By this time, it was no longer scandalous to be a non-conforming minister, and he was “associated with some of the best society of the metropolis[^hatfield-biography].” Like Watts, Stennett would have been welcomed into the Church of England, but held to his nonconformity. His works were eventually published in three volumes. These were mostly sermons and practical theology, but he also wrote a book-length defense of believer’s baptism. He was awarded an honorary Doctor of Divinity by King’s College and the University of Aberdeen.

Here is an example of his practical theology, from an essay on “Duties of servants to masters, is a commentary on Ephesians 6:5–8, which is very Lockean in spirit:

> But it is of the rights of masters and servants in a domestic capacity that we are here speaking. And these are, no doubt, the result of voluntary compact or agreement. No man has a right, because he possesses more than his neighbour, to compel him to become his servant. Nor has another a right, because of his poverty, to force his service on the rich[^free-servants].

But, of course, he is better known for his poetry. Verses of his poem, “The promised land” are used in five songs in _The Sacred Harp_ (here, punctuated as in the 1824 collection of his _Works_). None of them contains the entire poem, but using just the texts in the 2025 edition, you could recreate it.

On Jordan's stormy banks I stand,  
And cast a wishful eye  
To Canaan's fair and happy land.  
Where my possessions lie. [65, 128, 378t, 439, 442]

O the transporting rapt'rous scene  
That rises to my sight !  
Sweet fields, array'd in living green,  
And rivers of delight ! [65, 128, ~~378t~~, 439, 442]

There generous fruits, that never fail,  
On trees immortal grow :  
There rocks, and hills, and brooks, and vales,  
With milk and honey flow. [~~65~~, ~~128~~, ~~378t~~, ~~439~~, 442]

All o'er those wide extended plains  
Shines one eternal day ;  
There God the Sun[^sun-or-son] for ever reigns,  
And scatters night away. [~~65~~, ~~128~~, ~~378t~~, ~~439~~, 442]

No chilling winds, or pois'nous breath,  
Can reach that healthful shore :  
Sickness and sorrow, pain and death,  
Are felt and fear'd no more. [65, ~~128~~, ~~378t~~, ~~439~~, ~~442~~]

When shall I reach that happy place.  
And be for ever blest ?  
When shall I see my Father's face.  
And in his bosom rest? [~~65~~, ~~128~~, 378t, ~~439~~, ~~442~~]

Fill'd with delight, my raptur'd soul  
Can here no longer stay :  
Tho' Jordan's waves around me roll,  
Fearless I'd launch away. [~~65~~, 128, ~~378t~~, ~~439~~, ~~442~~]

On the other hand, his poem, “The converted thief” is taken over wholesale into _The Sacred Harp_ (44 THE CONVERTED THIEF). Here it is, again with its original punctuation and in common meter (not common meter double).

As on the cross the Saviour hung,  
And wept, and bled, and died,  
He pour'd salvation on a wretch  
That languish'd at his side.

His crimes, with inward grief and shame,  
The penitent confess'd;  
Then turn'd his dying eyes to Christ,  
And thus his pray'r address'd :

Jesus, thou Son and heir of heaven  
Thou spotless Lamb of God !  
I see thee bath'd in sweat and tears,  
And weltering in thy blood.

Yet quickly, from these scenes of woe,  
In triumph thou shalt rise,  
Burst through the gloomy shades of death,  
And shine above the skies.

Amid the glories of that world.  
Dear Saviour, think on me.  
And in the vict'ries of thy death  
Let me a sharer be.

His prayer the dying Jesus hears,  
And instantly replies,  
To-day thy parting soul shall be  
With me in Paradise.

Hugh McGraw used one verse of Stennett’s as the chorus to his 527 MY LIFE AND BREATH:

To Him I owe my life and breath  
And all the joys I have;  
He makes me triumph over death,  
And saves me from the grave.

The full poem has often been set to 68b ORTONVILLE, and may be Stennett’s most famous hymn text. For example, here are the verses found in Joseph Funk’s _Harmonia Sacra_:

Majestic sweetness sits enthron'd  
Upon his awful brow ;  
His head with radiant glories crown'd.  
His lips with grace o'erflow.

No mortal can with him compare,  
Among the sons of men:  
Fairer he is than all the fair  
That fill the heavenly train.

He saw me plung'd in deep distress.  
He flew to my relief;  
For me he bore the shameful cross,  
And carried all my grief.

To him I owe my life, and breath,  
And all the joys I have :  
He makes me triumph over death.  
And saves me from the grave.

131t MESSIAH uses the first two verses of _The Last Judgment_. It’s even more frightening in its complete form — at least, until the last two verses.

He comes ! he comes ! to judge the world,  
Aloud th' archangel cries !  
While thunders roll from pole to pole,  
And lightnings cleave the skies.

Th' affrighted nations hear the sound,  
And upward lift their eyes :  
The slumb'ring tenants of the ground  
In living armies rise.

Amid the shouts of numerous friends.  
Of hosts divinely bright,  
The Judge in solemn pomp descends,  
Array'd in robes of light.

His head and hairs are white as snow,  
His eyes a fiery flame,  
A radiant crown adorns his brow,  
And Jesus is his name.

Writ on his thigh his name appears.  
And scars his vict'ries tell :  
Lo — in his hand the conqu'ror bears  
The keys of death and hell.

So he ascends the judgment-seat,  
And at his dread command,  
Myriads of creatures round his feet  
In solemn silence stand.

Princes and peasants here expect  
Their last, their righteous doom ;  
The men who dar'd his grace reject,  
And they who dar'd presume.

Depart, ye sons of vice and sin,  
The injur'd Jesus cries !  
While the long-kindling wrath within  
Flashes from both his eyes.

And now in words divinely sweet,  
With rapture in his face,  
Aloud his sacred lips repeat  
The sentence of his grace :

Well done, my good and faithful sons.  
The children of my love !  
Receive the sceptres, crowns, and thrones,  
Prepar'd for you above.

Finally, Raymond Hamrick used the first and last verses of Stennett’s “The pleasures of social worship” for his 540 NIDRAH. I think the title, and the complete poem, helps us understand Stennett’s intention.

How charming is the place,  
Where my Redeemer God  
Unveils the beauties of his face,  
And sheds his love abroad !

Not the fair palaces,  
To which the great resort.  
Are once to be compar'd with this,  
Where Jesus holds his court.

Here, on the mercy-seat,  
With radiant glory crown'd,  
Our joyful eyes behold him sit,  
And smile on all around.

To him their prayers and cries  
Each humble soul presents :  
He listens to their broken sighs,  
And grants them all their wants.

To them his sov'reign will  
He graciously imparts ;  
And in return accepts, with smiles,  
The tribute of their hearts.

Give me, O Lord, a place  
Within thy blest abode.  
Among the children of thy grace,  
The servants of my God.

At his funeral, ministers of several different denominations provided remembrances and sermons[^funeral-denominations]. He was called “one of the best of men, and one of the best of ministers[^dan-taylor-eulogy]” by one of his eulogists. May he be remembered in the “blest abode / among the children of [God’s] grace.”

[^sacred-harp-songs]: 44 THE CONVERTED THIEF, 65 SWEET PROSPECT, 128 THE PROMISED LAND, 131t MESSIAH, 378t HEAVENLY PORT, 439 JORDAN, 442 NEW JORDAN, 527 MY LIFE AND BREATH, and 540 NIDRAH.

[^joseph-stennett-source]: Elizabeth Clarke, “Hymns, psalms, and controversy in the seventeenth century,” in _Dissenting Praise: Religious Dissent and the Hymn in England and Wales_, Isabel Rivers and David L. Wykes, editors. Oxford University Press.

[^pinners-hall]: Watts’s congregation had met for a while in the same building as the Sabbatarian Baptist church, Pinners’ Hall. Pinners’ Hall was host to a variety of non-conforming churches over the years.

[^hatfield-biography]: Edwin Hatfield, _The Poets of the Church_, 1884, quoted in the article on [Stennett](https://www.hymnologyarchive.com/samuel-stennett) in Hymnology Archive.

[^free-servants]: Slavery was still legal in the English colonies at this point, but Stennett is talking here of “free” servants, who have voluntarily become servants of others as employees.

[^sun-or-son]: “God the _Sun_”, edited to “God the _Son_” in _The Sacred Harp_. Rippon’s _Selection_ has “God the Sun” in the online versions I checked. The _Southern Harmony_ and the 1911 James edition of _The Sacred Harp_ have “God the Son.”

[^funeral-denominations]: Once again, just as happened with Isaac Watts. This was not an uncommon practice.

[^dan-taylor-eulogy]: Dan Taylor, “A good minister of Jesus Christ.” https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-good-minister-of-jesus_taylor-dan_1795, accessed October, 2026.
